CHAPTER 41 – Urinary Elimination
Definition
Urinary elimination is the regular removal of liquid waste (urine) from the body.​
It is essential for maintaining life and health.
Key Concepts (Very Important for Exam)
Urinary elimination is affected by:
●​ Food intake
●​ Fluid intake
●​ Medications
●​ Fluid loss (breathing, perspiration)
●​ Social and cultural habits
●​ Emotional needs
●​ Privacy​

⚠️Physical
In Fundamentals, always think:​
+ emotional + privacy needs

Normal Adult Output
Healthy adults eliminate approximately:

1400 mL of urine per day
(Depends on intake and losses)
Organs Required for Normal Elimination
Proper urinary elimination requires:
1.​ Kidneys
2.​ Ureters
3.​ Urinary bladder
4.​ Urethra
5.​ Pelvic floor muscles
All must function correctly.
Nursing Importance
Understanding normal anatomy and physiology is essential because nurses must:
●​ Assess elimination patterns
●​ Identify abnormal findings
●​ Prevent complications
●​ Promote normal function
●​ Protect dignity and privacy
High-Yield Galen Exam Points
●​ Privacy is part of elimination care
●​ Emotional needs matter
●​ 1400 mL/day = normal adult average
●​ Urinary system = kidneys → ureters → bladder → urethra
●​ Pelvic floor muscles are essential for continence​
NORMAL STRUCTURE AND FUNCTION OF THE URINARY SYSTEM (LO 41.1)
(Fundamentals – High-Yield Notes)
Overall Function of the Urinary System
The urinary system:
●​ Excretes water and electrolytes
●​ Removes waste products from blood
●​ Maintains blood composition
●​ Regulates blood pressure
●​ Maintains electrolyte balance
●​ Maintains acid–base balance
Important Waste Products
●​ Urea → from digestion of protein
●​ Creatinine → from muscle metabolism
These must be removed to prevent toxicity.
Location
●​ Two kidneys
●​ Bilateral
●​ Below ribs

●​ Mid-back area
Major Functions (Very Important for Exam)
Kidneys:
●​ Filter liquid waste from blood
●​ Balance electrolytes
●​ Regulate blood volume
●​ Regulate blood pressure
●​ Produce erythropoietin (RBC production)
●​ Synthesize vitamin D (calcium control)
●​ Maintain acid–base balance
Nephrons (Functional Unit)
Each kidney contains:
210,000 to 2 million nephrons
Each nephron includes:
1.​ Renal corpuscle
○​ Glomerulus
○​ Bowman capsule
2.​ Renal tubule
○​ Proximal tubule
○​ Loop of Henle
○​ Distal convoluted tubule
Urine Formation (3 Processes – Must Know)
1.​ Filtration
○​ Occurs in glomerulus
○​ Pressure forces fluid across membrane
2.​ Reabsorption
○​ Occurs in renal tubule
○​ Most filtrate returns to blood
3.​ Secretion
○​ Waste, excess solutes, water remain
○​ Urine is formed
URETERS
●​ Narrow tubes
●​ Carry urine from kidneys to bladder
●​ Muscle walls contract and relax (peristalsis)
●​ Push urine downward
Backflow or retention → risk for kidney infection
Bladder
Location:
●​ Below umbilicus
●​ Above symphysis pubis
Function:
●​ Stores urine

⚠️

●​ Expands to hold urine
●​ Contracts to empty
Sphincter Muscles
●​ Located at base of bladder
●​ Prevent leakage
●​ Relax during urination
Urethra
●​ Transports urine outside body
Micturition (Urination)
Process:
1.​ Bladder stretches
2.​ Nerve signals create urge
3.​ Brain signals:
○​ Bladder contracts
○​ Sphincter relaxes
4.​ Urine exits
If sequence is disrupted → retention or incontinence
NORMAL URINE CHARACTERISTICS
Normal urine:
●​ Sterile
●​ Contains fluids, salts, waste
●​ No bacteria, viruses, fungi
Amount
Average adult:
~1400 mL/day
Depends on:
●​ Fluid intake
●​ Diet (salt)
●​ Medications
●​ Medical conditions
Nighttime
●​ Urine production decreases at night
●​ About half of daytime amount
With aging:
●​ Decreased ability to concentrate urine
●​ Increased nighttime urination

●​ Urgency

●​ Burning

Galen Exam High-Yield Points

●​ Kidneys produce erythropoietin

ABNORMAL URINATION PATTERNS
1. Anuria
Definition
Failure of kidneys to produce urine.
Amount

Less than 50–100 mL in 24 hours
Causes
●​ Decreased blood flow to kidneys
●​ Complete obstruction (stones, tumors)
●​ Blocked ureters or urethra
Serious Risk
●​ Acute kidney injury
●​ Chronic kidney disease
●​ Coma
●​ Death
Acute anuria = LIFE-THREATENING EMERGENCY

⚠️

Dialysis
Hemodialysis
●​ Blood flows through machine
●​ Waste filtered out
●​ Usually 3 times per week in ESRD
Peritoneal Dialysis
●​ Dialysis solution placed into abdomen
●​ Peritoneum acts as membrane
●​ Waste removed through catheter

Reduced urine output.
Values

●​ Adults/Children: < 0.5 mL/kg/hr

●​ Dehydration
●​ Low cardiac output
●​ Vascular collapse
Intrarenal:
●​ Kidney structural damage

●​ Hypotension
●​ Edema
●​ Anemia

●​ Hepatomegaly
●​ Hypertension

KEGEL EXERCISES (Pelvic Floor Exercises)
Definition
Kegel exercises are repeated contractions of the pelvic floor muscles to strengthen the muscles
that support the:
●​ Bladder
●​ Uterus
●​ Bowel
Used mainly to prevent and treat urinary incontinence (especially stress incontinence).
Anatomy – What Muscle?
Pelvic floor muscles (levator ani group) support pelvic organs and control urethral closure.
How to Identify the Muscle (Exam Tip)
Tell patient to:
●​ Tighten muscles as if stopping urine flow
●​ Or preventing passing gas
Do NOT routinely stop urine midstream (only to identify muscle once).

⚠️

How to Perform (Teach This Exactly)
1.​ Tighten pelvic floor muscles.
2.​ Hold contraction for 5–10 seconds.
3.​ Relax for 5–10 seconds.
4.​ Repeat 10–15 times per session.
5.​ Perform 3 times daily.
Can be done:
●​ Lying down
●​ Sitting
●​ Standing
No one should see movement of:
●​ Abdomen
●​ Thighs
●​ Buttocks
Only pelvic floor contracts.

When Improvement Occurs
Improvement usually seen in:
8–10 weeks with regular practice.
Consistency is key.

⏳

If Patient Has Difficulty
Biofeedback may be used:
●​ Small vaginal or rectal probe
●​ Or external electrodes
●​ Monitor shows correct muscle contraction
Helps ensure proper technique.

●​ Fewer than 3 bowel movements per week
●​ Difficult or infrequent bowel movements
What Happens Physiologically

●​ Slowed peristalsis
●​ Increased water absorption in colon
●​ Hard stool
●​ Excessive straining
Signs & Symptoms
●​ Hard feces
●​ Straining
●​ Rectal pain
●​ Abdominal cramping
●​ Pressure
●​ Distention
●​ Anorexia
●​ Headache
Causes of Constipation
Lifestyle
●​ Ignoring urge
●​ Low fiber diet
●​ High animal fat diet
●​ Low fluid intake
●​ Irregular bowel habits
●​ Bed rest
●​ Lack of exercise​
Neurologic
●​ Spinal cord injury
●​ Tumors​
Metabolic
●​ Hypothyroidism
●​ Hypercalcemia​
Hypokalemia​
Psychiatric
●​ Anxiety
●​ Depression
●​ Cognitive impairment​
Medications
●​ Anticholinergics
●​ Antispasmodics
●​ Anticonvulsants
●​ Antidepressants
●​ Antihistamines
●​ Antihypertensives

●​ Antiparkinsonian drugs
●​ Diuretics
●​ Antacids
●​ Iron supplements
●​ Calcium supplements
●​ Opioids​
⚠ Laxative misuse → rebound constipation
Older Adults
●​ Slowed peristalsis
●​ Reduced mucus
●​ Low fiber intake​
Hemorrhoids
●​ Pain → stool retention → worsens constipation
RISKS OF CONSTIPATION
Straining during defecation can cause:
●​ Increased intraocular pressure
●​ Increased intracranial pressure
●​ Cardiac rhythm changes
●​ Hemorrhoids
VALSALVA MANEUVER
Definition:​
Bearing down while holding breath.
What happens:
●​ Increased intrathoracic pressure
●​ Rapid rise in BP
●​ Sudden drop in BP​
Results:
●​ Dizziness
●​ Blurred vision
●​ Fainting​
⚠ High risk in cardiac patients
IMPACTION
Definition
Hard fecal mass in rectum or colon that cannot be expelled.
Cause
●​ Unresolved constipation
●​ Stool becomes wedged in bowel​
High-Risk Patients
●​ Debilitated

●​ Confused
●​ Unconscious
Signs & Symptoms
●​ No normal stool for several days
●​ Repeated urge to defecate
●​ Cardinal sign: continuous oozing of liquid stool
○​ Liquid stool leaks around hardened mass
●​ Loss of appetite
●​ Nausea
●​ Vomiting
●​ Abdominal distention
●​ Cramping
●​ Rectal pain​
Risk Factors
●​ Barium from radiologic exams
○​ Increases risk for impaction
○​ Encourage fluids after barium
○​ May need laxatives or enemas
Diagnosis
●​ Digital rectal exam
●​ Palpation of fecal mass​
Digital removal:
●​ Within nursing scope (depends on facility policy)
●​ May require PCP order
FLATULENCE
Definition
Gas produced in intestine during digestion.
Gas (flatus) expelled by:
●​ Mouth → belching
●​ Anus → passing gas
Causes
●​ Bacterial action in colon
●​ Swallowed air
●​ Gas diffusion from bloodstream
●​ Gas-forming foods:
○​ Cabbage
○​ Onions
●​ Abdominal surgery
●​ Narcotics

Signs & Symptoms
●​ Gas pressure
●​ Abdominal distention
●​ Sharp pain
●​ Audible sounds
Nursing Points
●​ Normal sign of bowel activity
●​ Document return of flatus after surgery
●​ Walking or rocking helps relieve gas
●​ Rectal tube may be needed if severe
BOWEL DIVERSIONS
Terminology
Ostomy
●​ Surgically created opening
●​ Exits onto skin​
Stoma
●​ Actual visible opening
●​ Slight protrusion of mucosa
●​ Should be:
○​ Dark pink to red
○​ Warm
○​ Moist
○​ Similar to lips
Purpose of Bowel Diversions
●​ Divert fecal material
●​ Temporary or permanent
●​ Used when normal passage through rectum is not possible
Stool Consistency Depends on Location
●​ Ileostomy → very liquid stool
●​ Descending colostomy → formed stool​
Because:​
Water reabsorption varies by location in bowel.
COLOSTOMY
Definition
Portion of colon brought through abdominal wall to allow stool passage.
Temporary Colostomy
●​ Allows lower colon to heal

●​ Often emergency surgery
●​ Can be reversed
●​ May have 1 or 2 openings
○​ One may discharge mucus only
Permanent Colostomy
●​ After colon resection (e.g., cancer)
●​ End (terminal) colostomy
●​ Distal portion removed or sewn closed
Types of Colostomies
Descending / Sigmoid Colostomy
●​ Most common
●​ Located LLQ
●​ Produce solid stool
●​ Frequency can be regulated
●​ May not need appliance at all times
●​ Odor usually controlled
Transverse Colostomy
●​ Upper abdomen (middle/right)
●​ Semiformed, liquid stool
●​ Malodorous
●​ No control over discharge
●​ Appliance worn at all times
Ascending Colostomy
●​ Right side abdomen
●​ Liquid stool
●​ Digestive enzymes present
●​ Cannot regulate
●​ Rare
Loop Colostomy
●​ Temporary
●​ Right abdomen
●​ One stoma, two openings
○​ Proximal → stool
○​ Distal → mucus
●​ Large stoma
●​ Difficult to manage
Double-Barrel Colostomy
●​ Two separate stomas
●​ Proximal → functional
●​ Distal → nonfunctional

●​ Bowel surgically severed
ILEOSTOMY
Definition
Opening in small intestine (ileum).
Stool Characteristics
●​ Frequent
●​ Liquid
●​ Cannot regulate
●​ Contains digestive enzymes
●​ Can damage skin
●​ Odor minimal (few bacteria)​
Nursing Priority
●​ Continuous appliance
●​ Skin protection
●​ Monitor fluid & electrolytes​
ILEOANAL POUCH (J-Pouch)
●​ Colon and most rectum removed
●​ Internal pouch created from ileum
●​ Attached to anus
●​ No stoma
●​ Anal sphincter intact
●​ Temporary ileostomy first for healing​
Also called:
●​ Pull-through
●​ Pelvic pouch
●​ J-pouch
KOCK POUCH
●​ Internal reservoir from ileum
●​ Valve created
●​ Stoma present
●​ Catheter inserted several times daily to drain feces
Good. Same order. Clean study notes. Fundamentals level. No extra content.

Influenced by:
●​ Age
●​ Environment
●​ Culture
●​ Diet

●​ Physical activity
●​ Emotional health
●​ Surroundings​
DIET
Daily food intake maintains peristalsis.
Fiber
High-fiber diet promotes normal elimination.
Sources:
●​ Whole grains
●​ Fresh fruits
●​ Vegetables​
Recommended intake:​
20–35 g fiber/day (adults)
Fiber:
●​ Adds bulk
●​ Keeps stool soft
●​ Flushes fats & waste
Gas-Producing Foods
●​ Onions
●​ Cauliflower
●​ Beans​
Effects:
●​ Intestinal distention
●​ Increased colon motility
●​ Flatulence
Food Intolerance
Example:
●​ Lactose intolerance​
Symptoms:
●​ Watery stool
●​ Diarrhea
●​ Cramps
●​ Flatulence
Fluid Intake
Increased fluids:
●​ Easier stool passage​
Low fluid intake:

●​ Slower stool movement
●​ More water reabsorption
●​ Hard stool
●​ Constipation risk
Foods That Affect Stool
Spicy foods → diarrhea, gas​
Cheese, pasta, eggs, lean meats → constipation​
Bran, prunes, figs, chocolate, alcohol → laxative effect
Regular mealtimes:
●​ Promote regular peristalsis
PHYSICAL ACTIVITY
Regular exercise:
●​ Promotes peristalsis
●​ Moves chyme through colon
●​ Maintains abdominal & pelvic muscle tone​
Lack of activity:
●​ Weak abdominal muscles
●​ Poor intra-abdominal pressure
●​ Constipation
PSYCHOLOGICAL FACTORS
Stress:
●​ Increases peristalsis
●​ Causes diarrhea, nausea, gas​
Stress-related diseases:
●​ Colitis
●​ Crohn’s disease
●​ Ulcers
●​ IBS​
Depression:
●​ Slows peristalsis
●​ Causes constipation​
PERSONAL HABITS
Influenced by:
●​ Privacy
●​ Bathroom availability
●​ Busy schedule
●​ Hospital environment
Hospital factors:

●​ Embarrassment (bedpan, shared toilets)
●​ Ignoring urge to defecate​

●​ Increased water absorption
●​ Hard stool
●​ Constipation
●​ Impaction​
Chronic suppression:
●​ Loss of urge reflex
POSTURE
Normal defecation posture:
●​ Squatting​
Immobility:
●​ Weak abdominal contraction
●​ Difficult defecation​
Nursing action:
●​ Elevate head of bed if allowed
PAIN
Causes suppression of urge:
●​ Hemorrhoids
●​ Rectal surgery
●​ Fistulas
●​ Abdominal surgery​
Repeated suppression:
●​ Constipation​
Narcotics:
●​ Slow peristalsis
●​ Cause constipation​

PREGNANCY
●​ Enlarged uterus presses on rectum
●​ Difficult stool passage
●​ Increased risk hemorrhoids
●​ Iron in prenatal vitamins → constipation

PHARMACOKINETICS
Therapeutic effect
●​ Desired result or action of a medication.​
To achieve a therapeutic effect, the medication must:
●​ Enter the body
●​ Be absorbed

●​ Be distributed to cells and tissues
●​ Alter physiologic functioning​
Effectiveness is influenced by:
●​ Medication dose
●​ Route of administration
●​ Frequency of administration
●​ Function of metabolizing organs (liver, kidneys)
●​ Age of the patient
●​ Prescribing practitioners and nurses monitor the patient’s response.
●​ Laboratory and diagnostic studies measure medication effects.
●​ Medication actions depend on:​
○​ Absorption
○​ Distribution
○​ Metabolism
○​ Excretion
Absorption
●​ Passage of a drug from the administration site into the bloodstream.​
Factors affecting absorption:
●​ Route of administration
●​ Ability of the drug to dissolve (solubility)
●​ Blood flow to the administration site
●​ Body surface area
●​ Patient age​
Rate of absorption (fastest to slowest):
1.​ Intravenous (IV)
2.​ Intramuscular (IM)
3.​ Subcutaneous (SubQ)
4.​ Oral (PO)
Distribution
●​ Process of delivering the medication to tissues and organs and ultimately to the specific
site of action.​
Distribution is affected by:
●​ Chemical properties of the drug
●​ Effectiveness of the cardiac system
●​ Ability to pass through tissue and organ membranes
●​ Extent of protein binding
●​ Accumulation in fatty tissue
Metabolism

●​ Process by which a drug is altered to a less active form to prepare for excretion.
●​ Products of metabolism are called metabolites.
●​ Most metabolism occurs in the liver.​
Metabolism may be slowed in:
●​ Elderly individuals
●​ Individuals with impaired liver function​
Risk:
●​ Toxic drug levels can build up if the liver cannot break down the drug properly.
Excretion
●​ Process that removes the less active drug or its metabolites.​
Most excretion occurs through:
●​ Kidneys​
Other routes:
●​ Feces
●​ Breath
●​ Saliva
●​ Sweat
●​ Breast milk​
Risk for accumulation:
●​ Elderly individuals
●​ Individuals with impaired kidney function​
May require:
●​ Smaller doses
●​ Longer intervals between doses
PHARMACODYNAMICS
●​ Drug activity results from chemical interactions between a medication and the cells of the
body, causing a biologic response.​
Biochemical response may be:
Systemic
●​ Example: Pain medication affecting:​
○​ Nervous system (sedation)
○​ Respiratory system (change in respiratory rate)
○​ Gastrointestinal system (constipation)​
Local
●​ Example: Antipruritic lotion applied to an insect bite
●​ Response is evaluated based on changes in the patient’s clinical condition.

●​ Improvement occurs when the therapeutic effect is achieved.
●​ Unexpected or unwanted effects may occur.
●​ Different patients may respond differently to the same medication.
Drug Level and Half-Life
●​ Desired drug action is produced by maintaining a constant drug level in the body.
●​ Based on the drug’s half-life.​
Half-life
●​ Time it takes for blood concentration to decrease to one-half of the original dose due to
metabolism and excretion.​
Example:
●​ If half-life is 12 hours → 50% remains in bloodstream after 12 hours.
●​ Repeated doses are usually required to maintain desired drug level.
●​ Correct spacing of doses is based on the drug’s half-life.
●​ Maintaining consistent drug levels is important to obtain therapeutic effects.
Other Drug Action Factors
Onset of action
●​ Time it takes for the body to respond after administration.
●​ Affected by:​
○​ Route
○​ Drug formulation
○​ Pharmacokinetic factors​
Example:
●​ Insulin onset varies depending on:
○​ Route (IV vs. subcutaneous)
○​ Type (lispro vs. glargine)

●​ Unintended
●​ Unwanted

●​ Metabolite​

DOSAGE CALCULATION
Three measurement systems:
1.​ Metric
2.​ Apothecary
3.​ Household​
Nurses must understand all three.
Metric System
Basic measures:
●​ Gram (weight)
●​ Liter (volume)
●​ Meter (linear)
●​ Based on units of 10.
●​ 1 g = 1000 mg​
Example:
●​ 0.5 g = 500 mg​
SAFE PRACTICE ALERT
●​ Always place a zero before decimal (0.5 mL).
●​ Never use trailing zero (5 mL, not 5.0 mL).
Apothecary System
●​ Older system.
●​ Basic unit of volume: minim
●​ Increasing order: dram, ounce, pint, quart, gallon
●​ Weight based on grain (gr).
●​ No longer used on drug labels.
●​ Metric system should be used.
Household System
●​ Least accurate.
●​ Base unit: drop (gtt)
●​ Increasing order:​
○​ Teaspoon (tsp)

○​ Tablespoon (tbsp)
○​ Cup (c)​
Conversions:
●​ 1 tsp = 5 mL
●​ 1 tbsp = 15 mL
Calculation Methods
●​ Identify:​
○​ Available quantity
○​ Desired (prescribed) quantity​
Dimensional Analysis
●​ Consistent method
●​ Used for:​
○​ Basic calculations
○​ Pediatric doses
○​ Critical care titrations​
Steps:
1.​ Identify required unit.
2.​ Start with prescribed dose.
3.​ Multiply by available concentration.
4.​ Cancel like units.
5.​ Multiply numerators.
6.​ Multiply denominators.
7.​ Divide numerator by denominator.​
Example:
Amoxicillin 0.5 g​
Available: 250 mg per capsule
Conversion:
●​ 0.5 g × 1000 mg / 1 g​
Final answer:
●​ 2 capsules per dose​
Using a consistent method reduces medication errors.
SAFE PRACTICE ALERT
●​ When writing a metric dose:​
○​ Always place a zero in front of the decimal point.
○​ Never use a trailing zero.​

Correct examples:
●​ 0.5 mL (not .5 mL)
●​ 5 mL (not 5.0 mL)​
